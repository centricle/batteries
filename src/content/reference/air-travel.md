Spare lithium batteries, including power banks, must travel in carry-on baggage, never in checked bags, with their terminals protected from short circuit. In the United States, the FAA limits lithium-ion batteries to 100 watt-hours (Wh) each, allows up to two spare batteries of 101 to 160 Wh with airline approval, and limits lithium metal (non-rechargeable) batteries to 2 grams of lithium each. Ordinary alkaline, NiMH and NiCd cells may go in carry-on or checked bags if they are protected from damage and short circuit.

## Who sets the rules

In the United States, passenger battery rules come from Department of Transportation hazardous materials regulations (49 CFR 175.10), which the FAA explains on its PackSafe pages. The FAA's passenger battery chart notes that TSA security, individual airline and international rules "may, at times, be more restrictive." Internationally, airlines follow the ICAO Technical Instructions and the IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations; IATA publishes a passenger guidance document based on the 67th (2026) edition of its regulations. This page states the FAA rule first and the IATA guidance where it differs. Airlines may impose stricter limits than either.

## How to calculate watt-hours

Lithium-ion limits are set in watt-hours, not milliamp-hours. The FAA says to multiply the battery voltage by the amp-hour rating; IATA defines the rating as rated capacity in ampere-hours multiplied by nominal voltage (Wh = Ah x V). To convert mAh to Ah, divide by 1,000.

The FAA's own example is a 12-volt battery rated at 8 Ah: 12 x 8 = 96 Wh.

A single cylindrical cell works the same way. An [18650](/18650) cell rated 3.6 V nominal and 3000 mAh:

- 3000 mAh / 1000 = 3.0 Ah
- 3.6 V x 3.0 Ah = **10.8 Wh**

That cell is well under the 100 Wh limit. IATA notes that lithium-ion batteries are required to carry a Wh rating on the case, and that an airline may refuse a battery or device if staff cannot verify the rating from the battery or its documentation.

## Limits at a glance (United States, FAA)

| Battery | Carry-on | Checked | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lithium-ion, spare, up to 100 Wh | Yes | No | Terminals protected |
| Lithium-ion, spare, 101 to 160 Wh | Yes, with airline approval | No | Two spares per person |
| Lithium-ion, over 160 Wh | No | No | Forbidden on passenger aircraft |
| Lithium metal, spare, up to 2 g lithium | Yes | No | Terminals protected |
| Power bank or charging case | Yes | No | Same Wh limits as spare lithium-ion |
| Device with installed lithium battery | Yes | Yes, if powered off and protected | Same Wh limits |
| Alkaline, NiMH, NiCd (AA, AAA, C, D, 9-volt, button) | Yes | Yes | Protected from damage and short circuit |

Sources: FAA PackSafe pages and the FAA's passenger batteries chart. For lithium metal batteries, the FAA allows larger batteries (2 to 8 grams) with airline approval; IATA limits that 2 to 8 gram range to batteries in, or spare for, portable medical electronic devices.

The FAA sets no quantity limit for most batteries carried for personal use; batteries carried for sale or distribution are prohibited. IATA's guidance sets a default limit of 15 portable electronic devices and 20 spare batteries per person, either of which the airline may raise.

## Spare batteries and terminal protection

"Spare" means a battery not installed in a device. The FAA requires spare lithium batteries to be in the cabin. When a carry-on bag is checked at the gate or planeside, the FAA requires all spare lithium batteries and power banks to be removed from the bag and kept with the passenger.

Battery terminals must not touch other metal. The FAA lists acceptable methods: leaving batteries in retail packaging, covering terminals with tape (non-metallic tape, for dry cells), using a battery case or a camera-bag battery sleeve, or placing each battery snugly in a plastic bag or pouch. The FAA states that leaving rechargeable batteries in their charging (wall) unit is not considered protection from short circuit.

## Power banks

Under FAA rules, power banks, portable chargers and phone charging cases are treated as spare lithium-ion batteries: carry-on only, 100 Wh per battery, or up to two of 101 to 160 Wh with airline approval. The FAA notes that many airlines set stricter limits on how many power banks a passenger may carry, regardless of Wh rating.

International rules changed in 2026. On 27 March 2026, ICAO adopted an addendum to its Technical Instructions that, according to IATA's operator guidance:

- separates power banks from spare batteries;
- limits each passenger to two power banks;
- prohibits recharging a power bank on board; and
- recommends that power banks not be used to recharge devices on board.

IATA's 2026 passenger guidance lists power banks over 100 Wh as forbidden and asks passengers not to store power banks in overhead lockers. The ICAO addendum itself still allows 100 to 160 Wh power banks with operator approval, but IATA states that provision will not be carried into the 68th edition of its Dangerous Goods Regulations, which takes effect on 1 January 2027.

IATA also notes that airlines prefer spare batteries and power banks to be at less than 25% charge, and that an operator approving multiple spare batteries may require extra separation between them or a state of charge below 25%.

## Devices in checked bags

The FAA says devices containing lithium batteries "should be carried in carry-on baggage." When they are checked, they must be completely powered off and protected against unintentional activation or damage. IATA specifies that "off" means not in sleep or hibernation mode. Electronic cigarettes and vaping devices are carry-on only under both the FAA and IATA, and IATA prohibits recharging them on board.

For baggage trackers, the FAA chart allows checked carriage when the lithium content does not exceed 0.3 grams or the lithium-ion rating does not exceed 2.7 Wh. Baggage equipped with lithium batteries (smart luggage) must be carried on unless the batteries can be removed; removed batteries follow the same restrictions as spare batteries.

## Alkaline, NiMH and NiCd batteries

The FAA's dry cell entry covers "typical non-lithium dry batteries (alkaline, nickel metal hydride, nickel cadmium, etc.)" in sizes such as [AA](/aa), [AAA](/aaa), [C](/c), [D](/d), button cells and [9-volt](/9v). They are allowed in carry-on and checked bags with no quantity limit, as long as they are protected from damage and short circuit.

## Damaged and recalled batteries

The FAA prohibits damaged or recalled batteries and battery-powered devices that are "likely to create sparks or generate a dangerous evolution of heat" in both carry-on and checked bags, unless the battery has been removed or made safe. The FAA asks passengers to notify the flight crew immediately if a battery or device is overheating, expanding, smoking or burning.
