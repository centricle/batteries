Alkaline batteries can leak potassium hydroxide, a caustic alkaline electrolyte, usually after they are fully discharged, left in a device for a long time, mixed with other batteries or exposed to heat. Energizer's safety data sheet lists potassium hydroxide at 4% to 8% of an alkaline cell's weight and instructs flushing exposed skin with lukewarm water for at least 15 minutes, with immediate medical attention for eyes. Duracell advises cleaning leaked residue from device contacts while wearing household gloves and glasses. Lithium-ion cells fail differently: Battery University describes a failing cell as hissing, bulging and leaking a flammable electrolyte.

## Why alkaline batteries leak

Wikipedia's battery leakage article attributes leaks to pathways that form between the inside of a battery and the outside, caused by factory or design defects, excessive gas generation or physical damage. For alkaline cells, it describes hydrogen gas being generated as a byproduct once the battery is spent or reaches the end of its shelf life; when enough pressure builds, the casing splits at the base or side and releases manganese oxide, zinc oxide, potassium hydroxide, zinc hydroxide and manganese hydroxide.

Energizer's alkaline safety data sheet notes the "normal evolution of hydrogen gas from alkaline batteries," and warns designers of water- or air-tight devices that the gas must be absorbed or allowed to escape. It also states that a prolonged short circuit causes the battery to lose energy as heat and can open the safety release vent.

Energizer and Duracell list these conditions as causes of leakage or rupture:

| Condition | Source |
|---|---|
| Leaving depleted batteries in a device that keeps drawing power | Duracell |
| Leaving batteries in a device unused for several months; many devices do not power off completely | Duracell |
| Mixing old and new batteries, or different types or brands | Energizer, Duracell |
| Recharging a battery not marked rechargeable | Energizer, Duracell |
| Inserting one battery backward in a device with three or more batteries | Duracell |
| Short circuit from loose batteries touching coins or other metal | Energizer, Duracell |
| Crushing, puncturing or taking a battery apart | Energizer |
| Extreme temperature cycling, which can cause seal failure | Energizer |

## Leaks and vents by chemistry

| Chemistry | What it can release | Source |
|---|---|---|
| [Alkaline](/chemistry/alkaline) | Potassium hydroxide electrolyte (4% to 8% by weight per Energizer; 5% to 10% per Duracell) | Energizer and Duracell safety data sheets |
| [Zinc-carbon](/chemistry/zinc-carbon) | Acidic ammonium chloride or zinc chloride electrolyte; prone to leak when fully discharged or after 3 to 5 years | Wikipedia |
| [NiMH](/chemistry/nimh) | Electrolyte seeping around the seals under prolonged load, or carried out through the resealable vent on overcharge | Energizer NiMH handbook |
| [NiCd](/chemistry/nicd) | Electrolyte through a degraded seal after age and thermal cycling | Wikipedia |
| [Lithium iron disulfide](/chemistry/lithium-iron-disulfide) | Internal pressure release through a vent ball; ether odor in storage is not leakage | Energizer lithium handbook |
| Lithium coin | Ether-like odor of dimethoxyethane through the seal after long storage, which Energizer says is not a safety concern | Energizer lithium coin handbook |
| [Lithium-ion](/chemistry/lithium-ion) | Flammable electrolyte (lithium salt in an organic solvent); a failing cell may hiss and bulge | Battery University BU-304a |

Energizer's NiMH handbook describes "creep leakage": when NiMH cells are stored under load, small amounts of electrolyte can seep around the seals or through the vent and form potassium carbonate crystals, and in extreme cases corrode the battery or device. If a NiMH cell is overcharged or used with an incompatible charger, oxygen and hydrogen can form faster than the cell recombines them, the vent opens to prevent rupture and then reseals, and the escaping gas can carry electrolyte that forms crystals or rust outside the can.

For lithium iron disulfide AA and AAA cells, Energizer states that the electrolyte odor that can occur during high-temperature operation or storage comes from ethers passing through the seal at a few parts per million, and "does not indicate leakage."

A swelling, hissing or leaking lithium-ion cell is a fire hazard rather than a cleanup job. See [lithium-ion cell safety](/reference/lithium-ion-safety).

## Protective equipment and first aid

Published guidance for leaked alkaline, NiCd and NiMH batteries:

- **Hands:** Energizer's safety data sheet says to use hand protection in case of rupture or leakage. Duracell recommends household gloves.
- **Eyes:** Duracell recommends glasses. Wikipedia's leakage article recommends eye protection and rubber gloves because potassium hydroxide is caustic.
- **Skin contact:** Energizer: flush exposed skin with flowing lukewarm water for at least 15 minutes, then wash with soap and water. Duracell: wash exposed body surfaces and clothing with soap and water.
- **Eye contact:** Energizer: get immediate medical attention. Duracell: flush the eyes with running water for at least 15 minutes and seek immediate medical help. Duracell's safety data sheet adds holding the eyelids open to assure thorough flushing.
- **Inhalation:** Duracell's safety data sheet states the contents of a leaking battery may irritate the respiratory passages; move to fresh air.

Duracell also advises limiting handling of a battery that is wet or covered with a white powdery substance to what is needed to remove and dispose of it.

## Cleaning a device

Duracell's published steps for cleaning up after a leak:

1. Work in a well-ventilated area.
2. Wear household gloves and glasses.
3. Remove the residue from the electrical contacts with a toothbrush or cotton swab.
4. For alkaline, NiCd and NiMH leakage, Duracell suggests one tablespoon of boric acid in one gallon of water, or diluted vinegar or lemon juice mixed 50/50 with water.
5. Make sure the device is completely dry before inserting a new battery.

For routine care, Energizer's handbook recommends keeping battery contact surfaces clean by gently rubbing them with a clean pencil eraser or cloth, and Duracell gives the same advice for battery and compartment contacts. For spills from a ruptured battery, Energizer's safety data sheet says to prevent skin contact and collect the released material in a plastic-lined metal container.

The Battery Network (formerly Call2Recycle) states that corroded or leaking alkaline batteries are not considered damaged or defective for recycling purposes, so the special handling it requires for damaged lithium batteries does not apply to them. Disposal rules are on the [recycling and disposal](/reference/recycling-and-disposal) page.

## Prevention

The steps manufacturers publish to reduce leakage:

- Replace all batteries in a device at the same time, with the same size and type (Energizer, Duracell).
- Remove batteries from devices that will be stored, and remove depleted batteries promptly (Energizer, Duracell).
- Inspect battery compartments every few months (Energizer).
- Store batteries in a cool, dry place at normal room temperature (Energizer, Duracell).
- Recharge only batteries marked rechargeable (Energizer).
- For NiMH cells in extended storage, Energizer suggests insulating tape over the positive terminal or removing the cells from the device.

In 1964, according to Wikipedia, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission barred dry-cell battery advertising and packaging from using the word "leakproof" or the phrase "guaranteed leakproof," having found that no manufacturer had made a battery truly impervious to leaking. The FTC repealed that ban in 1997.
