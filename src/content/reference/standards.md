In the United States, two battery standards are required by law: ANSI/UL 4200A-2023 for consumer products containing button or coin batteries, and UN 38.3 for lithium batteries in transport. Most of the others are voluntary unless a regulation or customer requires them. Consumer battery standards fall into three groups. Size and name standards (IEC 60086 parts 1 to 3 for single-use batteries, IEC 61951 and IEC 61960 for rechargeables, and the American ANSI C18 series) define sizes, names and test methods so that batteries from different makers fit the same devices. Safety standards (IEC 60086-4 and -5, IEC 62133, UL 1642, UL 2054 and ANSI/UL 4200A) set abuse tests and warnings. UN 38.3 is a transport test for lithium batteries and the cells they are built from.

Standards bodies write "primary" for single-use and "secondary" for rechargeable, and they treat a cell (one single unit) and a battery (one or more cells in a finished product) as different things. The scope column below keeps their wording.

## IEC 60086: single-use batteries

IEC 60086 is the international series for single-use batteries. The IEC's catalog lists eight documents in the current set: parts 1, 2-1, 2-2, 3, 4, 5 and 6, and technical report 7.

**Part 1** is the base standard. The IEC says its purpose is to ensure that batteries from different makers "are interchangeable according to standard form, fit and function." It defines the system letters, electrodes, electrolytes and voltages used in battery names (see [how battery names work](/reference/battery-naming)). The 2026 edition added a new system letter, "T," raised the maximum open-circuit voltage for letter F (lithium iron disulfide) from 1.83V to 1.90V, and added an annex identifying batteries of general use and the tests used to compare their performance "in support of Regulation (EU) 2023/1542."

**Part 2** was split in 2026. The commented versions of IEC 60086-2-1 and IEC 60086-2-2 both describe the changes they make to IEC 60086-2:2021, with aqueous-electrolyte batteries in part 2-1 and lithium batteries in part 2-2.

**Part 3** covers watch batteries. Wikipedia's battery nomenclature article notes that an appended "W" in a battery name states that the battery complies with all the requirements of IEC 60086-3, including dimensional tolerance, chemical leakage and test methods.

**Parts 4 and 5** are the safety standards, split by kind: part 4 for lithium, part 5 for aqueous electrolyte.

**Technical report 7**, published in 2026, gives a laboratory procedure for estimating a coin battery's potential to cause internal injury if swallowed. Medical guidance on swallowed batteries is in the [if a button or coin battery is swallowed](/reference/button-battery-ingestion) guide.

## Rechargeable batteries: IEC 61951, 61960 and 62133

The rechargeable standards are organized by kind. IEC 61951-1 (nickel-cadmium) and IEC 61951-2 (nickel-metal hydride) specify marking, designation, dimensions, tests and requirements for sealed cells "suitable for use in any orientation, for portable applications." IEC 61960-3 does the same for rechargeable lithium cells and defines the letter code described in [how battery names work](/reference/battery-naming).

IEC 62133 is the safety counterpart: part 1 for nickel systems and part 2 for lithium systems, each specifying "requirements and tests for the safe operation" of portable sealed cells and batteries under intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse. Battery University's BU-304b describes lithium-ion pack protection as governed by IEC 62133 (see [lithium-ion battery safety](/reference/lithium-ion-safety)).

Makers cite these standards in their own test claims. Panasonic, for example, states that its eneloop cycle-life and charge-retention figures come from internal testing to clauses of IEC 61951-2.

## ANSI C18

ANSI C18 is the American national series. Wikipedia's battery nomenclature article notes that the 12th edition of C18 began harmonizing with the IEC standard, so that an IEC R20 battery has the same dimensions as an ANSI 13. The CPSC's 2023 proposed rule on button batteries describes the scope split relevant to safety warnings: ANSI C18.1M is limited to aqueous kinds, including alkaline, and ANSI C18.3M, the "ANSI Safety Standard for Portable Lithium Primary Cells and Batteries," is limited to lithium. Reese's Law exempts button and coin batteries that comply with the marking and packaging provisions of ANSI C18.3M from its special-packaging requirement, but not from the labeling rule.

## UL 1642 and UL 2054

UL 1642 covers "primary (nonrechargeable) and secondary (rechargeable) lithium batteries for use as power sources in products," whether a single cell or several cells in series or parallel. Its stated purpose is to reduce the risk of fire or explosion when lithium batteries are used in a product, and the risk of injury when user-replaceable batteries are removed and discarded. It covers technician-replaceable batteries containing 5.0g or less of metallic lithium.

UL 2054 is the standard for household and commercial batteries. Intertek's notice for its third edition (2021) lists new construction requirements for wiring and terminals and revised requirements for adhesive labels, with an effective date of November 17, 2024. UL reports that the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has recognized both UL 2054 and UL 1642 as consensus standards for medical devices that use lithium or nickel-based batteries.

## ANSI/UL 4200A: products with button and coin batteries

ANSI/UL 4200A, "Standard for Safety for Products Incorporating Button Batteries or Coin Cell Batteries," is mandatory under U.S. law for consumer products that contain button or coin batteries. The CPSC incorporated the 2023 edition into 16 CFR part 1263, effective October 23, 2023, under Reese's Law. On November 18, 2026, the CPSC published a direct final rule adopting ANSI/UL 4200A-2026, effective February 13, 2027 unless it receives a significant adverse comment by December 18, 2026. The requirements are summarized in the [if a button or coin battery is swallowed](/reference/button-battery-ingestion) guide.

## UN 38.3: transport testing

UN 38.3 refers to Part III, sub-section 38.3 of the United Nations Manual of Tests and Criteria. In the United States, 49 CFR 173.185 requires each lithium cell or battery to be "of the type proven to meet the criteria" of sub-section 38.3, whether or not the cells used to build a battery were themselves tested. IATA states that all batteries carried by airline passengers must be of a type that meets UN 38.3, and adds that the test standard "is not a consumer product safety standard." Passenger rules are in the [flying with batteries](/reference/air-travel) guide.
