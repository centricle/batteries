A swallowed button or coin battery can cause severe burns if it lodges in the esophagus, and the National Capital Poison Center states that serious damage can occur in as little as 2 hours. If anyone swallows a battery, the Poison Center's instruction is to call the 24-hour National Battery Ingestion Hotline at 1-800-498-8666 or Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 immediately, without waiting for symptoms. An x-ray is usually needed to find where the battery is. This page summarizes what those authorities publish; it is not a substitute for their advice.

## Why a swallowed battery is dangerous

The National Capital Poison Center (NCPC) explains that most swallowed button batteries pass through the body, but a battery that gets "hung up," especially in the esophagus, can cause tissue damage. An electrical current forms around the outside of the battery and generates hydroxide, an alkaline chemical, which burns the surrounding tissue. It is not possible to tell from outside the body whether a swallowed battery will pass or lodge.

NCPC's clinical guideline identifies the 20 mm lithium coin cell, such as the [CR2032](/cr2032), as the size most frequently involved in esophageal injuries. Its diameter falls between a U.S. penny (19 mm) and a nickel (21 mm). Smaller cells lodge less often but can still cause serious injury or death, especially in children younger than 1 year.

NCPC reports that more than 3,500 people of all ages swallow button batteries each year in the United States.

## Signs to watch for

NCPC notes that most serious battery ingestions are not witnessed. Its guideline asks clinicians to consider battery ingestion in any patient with:

- airway obstruction, wheezing or noisy breathing
- drooling
- vomiting
- chest pain or discomfort
- abdominal pain
- difficulty swallowing
- decreased appetite or refusal to eat
- coughing, choking or gagging when eating or drinking

A person with a battery in the esophagus may have no symptoms at first. NCPC states: "Do not wait for symptoms to develop." After a battery has passed into the stomach, NCPC lists fever, abdominal pain, vomiting and blood in the stools as symptoms to report immediately.

## What to do

These steps are quoted or paraphrased from NCPC's public page and its triage guideline.

1. **Call immediately.** National Battery Ingestion Hotline, 1-800-498-8666, available 24 hours a day; or Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222, which America's Poison Centers describes as the national line serving its 53 accredited U.S. poison centers. NCPC also offers an online tool, webPOISONCONTROL.
2. **Identify the battery if it is quick to do.** NCPC asks for the battery's identification number from the package or a matching battery. For a person with no symptoms, the guideline allows up to 5 minutes to find the code.
3. **Expect an x-ray.** NCPC states that in most cases an x-ray must be taken right away to confirm the battery has passed the esophagus. A battery that remains in the esophagus must be removed immediately.
4. **Nothing by mouth except honey (where it applies).** Other than honey, NCPC says not to eat or drink until an x-ray shows the battery is beyond the esophagus.
5. **If the battery passes on its own,** check the stools until it has passed.

For a battery in the nose or ear, NCPC says not to use nose or ear drops until a physician has examined the person, because the fluids can cause additional injury.

## Honey and sucralfate guidance (NCPC)

NCPC's guideline recommends honey at home and on the way to the emergency room when all of these conditions are met:

- a lithium coin cell may have been swallowed (if the type is unknown, NCPC says to assume a lithium coin cell unless it is a hearing aid battery);
- the child is **12 months of age or older**, because honey is not safe for children under one year;
- the battery was swallowed **within the prior 12 hours**;
- the child is able to swallow; and
- honey is immediately available.

The dose NCPC gives is 10 mL (2 teaspoons) of honey by mouth every 10 minutes, for up to 6 doses. NCPC advises commercial honey rather than specialized or artisanal honey, and states that honey "is NOT a substitute for immediate removal" of a lodged battery: it slows injury but does not stop it. NCPC also says: "Do not delay going to an ER."

Sucralfate (Carafate) appears in the same guideline as a hospital treatment. NCPC directs clinicians to give sucralfate suspension (1 g/10 mL), 10 mL every 10 minutes for up to 3 doses, after an x-ray shows the battery is lodged in the esophagus and before sedation for removal, if the child can swallow. Honey may be substituted in children 12 months or older. NCPC says not to give either if the battery may have been in the esophagus for more than 12 hours. NCPC bases both recommendations on a 2018 animal study (Anfang et al.) in which honey and sucralfate reduced the pH rise and the depth of esophageal injury.

## U.S. law: Reese's Law

Reese's Law (Public Law 117-171) was signed on August 16, 2022. It addresses the risk of injury to children 6 years old and younger from swallowing button cell or coin batteries. Federal regulations define a button cell or coin battery as a single-cell battery with a diameter greater than its height.

### Battery packaging

Section 3 of Reese's Law took effect by statute on February 12, 2023. According to the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), it requires button and coin batteries sold in the United States, including those packaged separately with a product, to be in child-resistant special packaging that meets 16 CFR 1700.15. The law exempts batteries that comply with the marking and packaging provisions of ANSI C18.3M, the American lithium primary battery safety standard; those batteries are still subject to the labeling rules.

CPSC's packaging label rule (16 CFR 1263.4) applies to battery packages manufactured or imported after September 21, 2024. It requires a warning label with an icon at least 8 mm in diameter on the principal display panel (if space does not allow the full label, a 20 mm icon on the principal panel and the warning text on a secondary panel), and these statements on the principal or secondary display panel:

- "Keep in original package until ready to use."
- "Immediately dispose of used batteries and keep away from children. Do NOT dispose of batteries in household trash."

### Products that use button and coin batteries

CPSC adopted ANSI/UL 4200A-2023 as the mandatory standard for consumer products that contain or use button or coin batteries, effective October 23, 2023. As described in the Federal Register, the standard requires:

- battery compartments that need a tool, or at least two independent and simultaneous hand movements, to open;
- abuse testing of the compartment, including drop, impact, compression, torque and tension tests, the last three adapted from the ASTM F963 toy standard;
- a secureness test for batteries secured to the product by soldering, fasteners or equivalent means; and
- warnings on the product where practicable, on its packaging and in its instructions.

Two categories are outside 16 CFR part 1263: zinc-air batteries, which CPSC has determined do not present an ingestion hazard, and toys that comply with the battery requirements of the toy standard at 16 CFR part 1250.

On November 18, 2026, CPSC published a direct final rule to adopt a revision, ANSI/UL 4200A-2026. The rule takes effect on February 13, 2027, unless CPSC receives a significant adverse comment by December 18, 2026. CPSC describes the changes as making the safety alert symbol at least as tall as the word "WARNING" and allowing the alternative product marking to be molded or stamped in a single color.

Hearing aid batteries ([13](/13), [312](/312)) are zinc-air cells, a chemistry Energizer's zinc-air handbook describes for hearing aid service. The exemption above concerns the federal product rule, not the medical guidance. NCPC's guideline handles a swallowed hearing aid battery as a small battery (12 mm or less) and still directs callers to the hotline.
